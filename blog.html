Music & the Developing Brain

Connection, creativity, and growth in action — how early childhood music shapes young minds.

We all know music can move us — but in early childhood, it does much more than that. For infants, toddlers, and preschoolers, music is a tool that shapes the brain, supports development, and builds lifelong skills. At Musical Sprouts, we use music intentionally — not just for entertainment, but as a brain-based, relationship-driven experience that supports the whole child.

Music builds brains — literally

In The Brain That Changes Itself, Norman Doidge shows how the brain is not fixed but constantly rewiring in response to experience — especially in early childhood, when neuroplasticity is at its peak. Music is one of the most powerful stimuli for this rewiring. It activates multiple areas of the brain at once: auditory, motor, emotional, linguistic, and executive function regions all light up during musical play.

This means that when your child claps to a beat, sings a simple melody, or dances with scarves, they’re not just having fun — they’re building new neural pathways tied to coordination, memory, language, and self-regulation.

Music supports the social brain

In I Heard There Was a Secret Chord, Hanif Abdurraqib reflects on music as a collective, connective force — something that bridges individual emotion with shared experience. The same is true for children. Musical experiences, especially in group settings, promote social-emotional development by creating safe, shared moments of joy, attention, and expression.

Babies bond through lullabies. Toddlers learn empathy by mirroring movements and rhythms. Preschoolers build group awareness through call-and-response and turn-taking songs. These experiences build emotional intelligence and foster secure attachment — key foundations for lifelong mental health.

Music builds language, literacy & numeracy

Songs are structured with rhythm, repetition, and rhyme — the same building blocks as early language and math. Children who engage in music-rich environments show stronger phonological awareness (a predictor of reading skills), better pattern recognition, and improved auditory memory.

Studies support this: musical play is linked to better literacy and numeracy outcomes in early childhood. And because musical learning is embodied — involving movement, voice, and play — it supports whole-brain integration far more than passive instruction.

The takeaway: music isn’t extra — it’s essential

At Musical Sprouts, we believe music is not a bonus activity. It’s a foundation for growth, grounded in research and delivered with joy. By creating a space where children and their caregivers sing, move, and connect, we’re supporting not just developmental milestones — but the architecture of the brain itself.

Music grows brains. Music grows connection. And most of all, music grows kids who are ready to engage with the world — creatively, confidently, and compassionately.
